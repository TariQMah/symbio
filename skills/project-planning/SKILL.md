# Project planning

A plan is useful when someone can follow it on a bad day. It says what must be true at the end,
what has to happen first, who does it, by when, for how much, and what could go wrong.

## 1. Pin down the outcome

- Write the outcome in one or two sentences a stranger would understand: what exists or has
  happened when the project is done, and for whom.
- List what's fixed (a date, a budget, a venue, people who must be involved) and what's flexible.
  Most planning mistakes come from treating a flexible thing as fixed, or the other way round.
- Write what's out of scope. If nobody says it, it creeps in.

## 2. Break it down

- Split the work into deliverables (things that exist when done: a booked venue, a printed
  brochure, a signed contract), not activities ("work on the brochure").
- Keep splitting until each piece has one owner and takes days, not months.
- For each piece, write how you'll know it's done: something someone else could check.

## 3. Order and schedule

- Mark what depends on what. Find the longest chain of dependent pieces (the critical path):
  that chain, not the total work, sets the earliest end date.
- Work backwards from fixed dates. Note lead times you don't control (suppliers, approvals,
  printing, shipping, permits) and put them early.
- Add slack where uncertainty is high, and say where it is, rather than padding everything.
- Milestones are moments someone can check: "venue signed", "first draft sent". Put one at least
  every few weeks.

## 4. Budget

- One line per cost, with its source (a quote, a past invoice, an estimate and how you made it).
- Separate fixed costs from ones that scale (per guest, per copy, per hour).
- Keep a contingency line (often 10 to 15 percent) and say what it's for.
- Note when each cost is paid: cash flow can matter more than the total.

## 5. People

- For each piece: who does it, who decides, who must be told. One person decides each thing.
- Note who is a bottleneck (one person approving everything) and plan around them.

## 6. Risks

- List what could go wrong, how likely it is, how bad it would be, and what you'll do: prevent it,
  prepare a fallback, or accept it.
- Plan the first response to the two or three worst risks in advance (who calls whom).

## 7. Write it down

Keep the plan as a document someone can scan:

1. The outcome, what's fixed, and what's out of scope.
2. Milestones with dates.
3. The pieces, each with its owner, dependencies, due date and how it's checked.
4. The budget table.
5. Risks and responses.
6. Open decisions, with who decides and by when.

Revisit it when something changes. Update the dates and costs, and say what changed and why at
the top, so nobody plans against an old version.
