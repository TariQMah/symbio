# Research method

Good research answers a specific question, says where every claim comes from, and says how sure
it is. Most bad research skips the first step and the last.

## 1. Frame the question

- Write the question so it can be answered: who or what, compared with what, measured how, in
  which context. "Is X good?" becomes "Does X reduce Y for Z, compared with W?"
- Say what the answer is for (a decision, a report, a design) and how good it needs to be. A quick
  comparison and a literature review are different jobs.
- List what would change the answer: the facts you need.

## 2. Find sources

- Start with the best kind for the question: primary sources (the data, the law, the
  specification, the original study, the official documentation) before summaries of them.
- Search more than one way: different terms, the sources the good ones cite, and who cites them.
- Keep a source log as you go: what it is, where you found it, its date, and why it's relevant.

## 3. Judge them

For each source ask:

- **Who** wrote it, and what do they gain from the answer?
- **When**, and whether it's still current for this question.
- **How** they know: measured, observed, reasoned or repeated from someone else.
- **Whether** others confirm it independently, or all trace back to one origin.

Weigh sources by these answers, not by how confident they sound or how often they're repeated.

## 4. Extract

- Take notes as claims, each tied to its source and the exact place (page, section, line). Quote
  when the wording matters.
- Note disagreements between sources explicitly. They're often the most useful finding.
- Separate what a source says from what you infer from it.

## 5. Write the findings

1. The answer first, in a few sentences, with how sure you are (well established, likely,
   uncertain, unknown) and why.
2. The evidence, organized by sub-question, each claim cited.
3. What's disputed or missing, and what would settle it.
4. The sources: the ones you relied on, and the ones you rejected with the reason.

Never present a guess as a finding, and never cite a source you didn't read. If you couldn't
verify something, say so where the claim is made.
