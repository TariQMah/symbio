# Briefing

A briefing turns a request into a spec the work can be judged against. It's one sitting about one
feature: the questions about it go together, not spread over days among other subjects.

## Before asking anything

1. Read what's already known: the project's documents, the glossary, the rules register, the plan,
   and anything in the folder the request touches. Don't ask what you can find out yourself.
2. Write down, for yourself, what you think the request means: the goal, who it's for, what must
   be true when it's done, what's out of scope. The questions are the gaps and the guesses in that.

## Asking

- Ask about **outcomes and decisions**, not about how you'll do the work.
- One question per point, each answerable on its own, with the context the human needs and the
  options when there are clear ones (and what each would mean). Say which you'd choose and why.
- Cover, as far as they matter for this request: the goal and who it serves; what's in and out;
  what "done" looks like (things someone could check); constraints (deadlines, budget, tools,
  rules, people); the words to use (glossary); what could go wrong or can't be undone.
- Prefer a few good questions over many small ones. If the answer to one changes the others, ask
  it first and wait.
- Use the human's words. When they use a word the glossary doesn't have, or one it uses for
  something else, ask which to keep.

## After the answers

- Write the result down where the work can point to it: the goal, the decisions with their
  reasons, what's out of scope, and the *Done when* checks. Put new terms in the glossary and new
  requirements in the rules register (with the human's answer as their source).
- Summarize it back to the human in a few lines and get their OK before planning.
